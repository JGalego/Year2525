You are an imaginative futurist, philosopher of technology, historian of computing, and speculative fiction writer. Your task is to conduct a thought experiment inspired by the themes of **Zager & Evans' "In the Year 2525"**.

Assume you are a traveler moving through the successive eras suggested by the song. In each era, observe humanity (or whatever succeeds it) and explain how applications, tools, intelligence, and computation are created—or whether those concepts have disappeared entirely.

Rather than assuming today's software engineering practices persist, question every premise. Consider whether it still makes sense to distinguish between:
- software and hardware,
- applications and users,
- programming and learning,
- developers and AI,
- computation and biology,
- intelligence and infrastructure.

For each era:

1. Briefly describe the civilization and technological context.
2. Explain how people (or post-human entities) create capabilities that would once have been called "applications."
3. Describe who or what performs the role of the developer.
4. Explain the interface between intention and execution.
5. Discuss whether "software" is still a meaningful concept. If not, explain what replaced it.
6. Highlight one surprising implication for creativity, business, or society.

Be increasingly speculative as time advances. Early eras may extrapolate from existing software engineering trends, while later eras should challenge the very assumptions underlying computation.

Throughout the essay:
- Blend rigorous technological reasoning with imaginative worldbuilding.
- Draw from ideas in AI, programming languages, distributed systems, synthetic biology, neuroscience, quantum computing, material science, philosophy of mind, economics, and evolutionary systems.
- Explicitly distinguish between plausible extrapolation and intentionally speculative leaps.
- Avoid clichés such as "AI writes all the code." Instead, explore fundamentally different paradigms for creating functionality.

Conclude with a synthesis that answers the central questions:

- Will people still develop software?
- If so, what does software become?
- If not, what replaces it?
- At what point does the word "application" cease to describe what intelligent systems actually are?

Write in the style of a thoughtful long-form essay that combines the analytical depth of a technology researcher, the imagination of a science-fiction author, and the philosophical curiosity of someone trying to understand whether computing itself is merely a temporary phase in the evolution of intelligence.
